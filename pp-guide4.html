Section 01
Introduction
Set the stage. Tell the reader what your project is about, why it matters, and what problem it addresses.
What is the Introduction?

The Introduction is the first impression of your entire proposal. It gives the reader the background context, the domain of your project, a hint of the problem, and why your project is important. Think of it as the "bigger picture" opening.

What to Include — 4 Key Elements
🌐

A — Background Context

Describe the industry or domain your project belongs to (e.g., education sector, healthcare, e-commerce). Explain the current state of the field — what technology or process currently exists. This gives the reader a foundation to understand your project's relevance.

❗

B — Problem Hint

Briefly mention the core problem your project is trying to solve. Don't go into full detail here — save that for Section 4. Just a short, punchy mention that makes the reader curious and engaged.

🚀

C — Project Name + Purpose

State the name of your application/system and write one clear sentence about what it does. This is your "one-liner" — make it memorable and precise.

🎯

D — Target Impact

Who benefits from this project? How will it impact them? Mention the specific user groups (students, teachers, doctors, shopkeepers, etc.) and the improvement it brings to their lives or work.

MeroAttendance — Student Attendance App:

"Student attendance tracking is a critical management task in the education sector. In Nepal, colleges still rely on daily manual attendance systems that waste valuable class time. MeroAttendance is a QR-code based digital attendance system designed for Pokhara University BCA students that can complete attendance tracking in under 30 seconds. This project will improve the productivity of teachers, students, and administrators."

💡

Pro Tip: Your opening sentence should grab attention. Start with a fact, a statistic, or a strong statement about the problem domain — not with "This project is about..."

🎯
Section 02
Aim & Scope
Define the overall goal and set clear boundaries — what your project will and will not do.
Aim

The Aim is the single, overarching goal of your project. It answers the question: "What is this project ultimately trying to achieve?"


Write 1–2 sentences. Include: what you are building, for whom, using what approach, and the expected improvement.

The aim of [Project Name] is to [action verb + what] for [target users] using [technology/method], improving [metric] by at least [X%].
Scope

The Scope defines the exact boundaries of your project. It prevents scope creep and tells the reader exactly what is included and what is excluded.


✅ IN SCOPE (What you WILL build)

Features and modules included in this version of the system.

❌ OUT OF SCOPE (What you WON'T build — maybe v2)

Features explicitly excluded from this version.

Aim Example:
"The aim of MeroAttendance is to digitize student attendance tracking in colleges using QR code technology, reducing manual effort and improving attendance accuracy by at least 95%."

✅ IN SCOPE

Student login & registration, QR code check-in, Teacher dashboard, Attendance reports, Admin panel

❌ OUT OF SCOPE

Parent SMS notifications, AI-based predictions, Blockchain certificates, Payroll integration

💡

Pro Tip: Out-of-scope items are not failures — they are future features (v2, v3). Label them clearly as "planned for future development" to show you've thought ahead.

✅
Section 03
Objectives
Write 3–5 specific, measurable goals using the SMART framework — each with a clear timeline and success metric.
The SMART Framework
Letter Stands For What It Means Example
S Specific Clearly define what you will do. Avoid vague verbs like "improve" alone. "Build a QR-code attendance check-in system"
M Measurable Include a number — students, %, seconds, users, features. "...working for minimum 100 students"
A Achievable Realistic within your project's resources and time. Don't over-promise. Don't say "10 million users" for a college project
R Relevant Directly related to your project's aim and the problem being solved. Each objective must tie back to your aim
T Time-bound State a specific deadline — "By Week 8", "By Month 3", etc. "...by Week 8 of development"
3 Sample SMART Objectives
1 Develop Core Attendance Module

"By Week 8, build a QR-code based attendance check-in system working for a minimum of 100 students across 5 classes."

Specific Measurable: 100 students, 5 classes Time-bound: Week 8
2 Create Teacher Dashboard

"By Week 9, implement a dashboard where teachers can view student-wise attendance, class-wise attendance %, and export reports to Excel."

Specific: 3 features listed Achievable Time-bound: Week 9
3 Conduct Usability Testing

"By Week 9, perform usability testing with 20+ students and achieve an 80%+ satisfaction score on the SUS scale."

Measurable: 80% score, 20 testers Relevant to project quality Time-bound: Week 9
⚠️

Common Mistakes: "The objective is to make a good app" — this is NOT SMART. There's no metric, no deadline, and it's not specific. Every objective must answer: What exactly? How many/much? By when?

🔥
Section 04
Problem Statement & Proposed Solution
The heart of your proposal. Convince the reader that the problem is real, serious, and that your solution uniquely addresses it.
Problem Statement

Describe the current pain point in detail. Use real numbers. Name the affected users. Explain the consequence of NOT solving it.

"Currently, [target users] face [specific problem] when [situation/context].

This causes [impact: time wasted, money lost, errors, etc.].

There is no existing solution that [gap being addressed]."
Proposed Solution

Describe your app and exactly how it solves the problem. Mention what makes it different from existing solutions.

[App Name] is a [web/mobile/desktop] application that [1-line about what it does].

It helps [target users] by [specific benefits].

Unlike [existing alternative], our solution [unique value]."
Full Worked Example
📋 MEROATTENDANCE — COMPLETE PROBLEM + SOLUTION
PROBLEM STATEMENT

"Currently, Pokhara University BCA students face inefficient manual attendance tracking when their teachers take roll call before each class. This causes 5–10 minutes of wasted class time daily, resulting in 1–2 hours lost per week per class. No existing solution tracks attendance in under 30 seconds while also preventing proxy attendance."

PROPOSED SOLUTION

"MeroAttendance is a QR-code based web application that lets students check in to classes in under 30 seconds. It helps students and teachers by eliminating manual roll call and providing instant attendance analytics. Unlike paper-based systems, our solution prevents proxy attendance via time-limited, unique QR codes that expire after 2 minutes."

💡

Key Rule: The problem and solution must be mirror images of each other. Every pain point mentioned in the problem must be directly addressed in the solution. If you mention "5–10 minutes wasted," your solution must state "under 30 seconds."

📌
Section 05
High-Level Requirements
Define what your system will do (functional), how well it will perform (non-functional), and prioritize features using MoSCoW.
5.1 Functional

"What does the app DO?" — concrete features and capabilities users can interact with.

  • → User login / registration
  • → CRUD operations on data
  • → Search and filter functions
  • → Generate reports / exports
  • → Notifications / alerts
📝

Minimum 8 functional requirements in your full proposal (Week 2)

5.2 Non-Functional

"How WELL does the app do it?" — quality attributes, constraints, and system-wide properties.

  • → Performance: page load < 2s
  • → Security: encrypted passwords
  • → Usability: mobile-friendly UI
  • → Scalability: 500+ concurrent users
  • → Availability: 99% uptime
📝

Minimum 5 non-functional requirements with measurable values

5.3 MoSCoW Priority

Prioritize requirements so everyone knows what gets built first and what can wait.

MUSTCore features — cannot ship without
SHOULDImportant, include if possible
COULDNice to have, low priority
WON'TNot in this version (future)
MoSCoW Table Example
Priority Feature / Requirement Reason
MUST HAVE Student login, QR code generation, Attendance check-in, Teacher dashboard Core system cannot work without these
SHOULD HAVE Attendance reports export, Email notifications, Admin dashboard Improves usability significantly
COULD HAVE Dark mode UI, Multi-language support, Analytics graphs Nice enhancement, not critical
WON'T HAVE AI prediction engine, Parent mobile app, Blockchain certificates Out of scope for v1 — planned for v2

Section 06
Design
Include UML diagrams, an Entity Relationship Diagram, and a Data Flow Diagram to visually describe your system architecture.
👤

Use Case Diagram

Shows who (actors) does what (use cases) in the system. Visualizes the interaction between users and system features.

Actors (users) Use cases Relationships <<include>> <<extend>>
📐

Use draw.io or Lucidchart. Include all user roles.

🗃️

ERD — Entity Relationship Diagram

Shows your database structure — all tables (entities), their attributes, and how they relate to each other.

Entities (tables) Attributes Primary Keys Foreign Keys Relationships (1:N, M:N)
📐

Minimum 4–5 entities. Show cardinality clearly.

🔄

DFD — Data Flow Diagram

Shows how data flows through your system — from input to processing to storage to output. Covers Level 0 (context) and Level 1 (detail).

Level 0 (Context) Level 1 (Detailed) Processes Data stores External entities
📐

Level 0 = one process bubble. Level 1 = expanded processes.

Diagram Checklist
  • ✓
    Figure Number + Caption
    Every diagram must have: "Figure 1: Use Case Diagram of MeroAttendance System"
  • ✓
    Legend / Key
    Include a legend explaining symbols used in each diagram type
  • ✓
    High Resolution
    Export diagrams as PNG at minimum 150 DPI — blurry diagrams lose marks
  • ✓
    Referenced in Text
    Write "As shown in Figure 1..." before each diagram — never insert a diagram without context
  • ✓
    Consistent Notation
    Use standard UML notation throughout — don't mix different symbol styles

Section 07
Development Cost Estimation
Break down all costs — hardware, software, and human resources — using realistic Nepalese market rates in NPR.
3 Cost Categories
🖥️

Hardware Costs

Computers, servers, networking equipment, testing devices. Include depreciation if equipment already owned.

💻

Software / License Costs

IDEs, cloud hosting (AWS/Heroku), domain, SSL certificates, design tools. Specify if free/open-source.

👨‍💻

Human Resource Costs

Developer hours × hourly rate. Break down by role (developer, designer, tester). NPR 500–1,500/hr typical.

Pro Tip: Always add a 10–15% miscellaneous/contingency buffer to your total. Real projects always have unexpected costs. This shows professional planning to your evaluator.

Section 08
Detail Timeline
A week-by-week Gantt chart showing what tasks happen when across your 10-week development period.
What is a Gantt Chart?

A Gantt chart is a horizontal bar chart showing tasks on the Y-axis and time (weeks) on the X-axis. Each bar represents when a task starts and ends. Overlapping bars show parallel work. It's the standard tool for project scheduling.

Sample 10-Week Timeline
TASK W1 W2 W3 W4 W5 W6 W7 W8 W9 W10
💡

20% Buffer Rule: Always add 1–2 buffer weeks. If your actual development takes 8 weeks, show 10 on your chart. Real projects always face delays.

🛠️

Tools: Use Microsoft Project, GanttProject (free), draw.io, or even Excel to create your Gantt chart. Export as PNG for the proposal.

✨
Section 09
Conclusion
Wrap up your proposal in 1–2 paragraphs, restating the project's value and expected outcomes.
What to Write in the Conclusion
  • ✓
    Restate the project's purpose — one sentence summary of what you built and why
  • ✓
    Summarize key features — 2–3 highlights from your solution
  • ✓
    Expected outcomes / impact — what will change after your project is deployed?
  • ✓
    Future work mention — briefly mention what could be added in v2
🚫

Do NOT include in Conclusion: New information not mentioned before, new diagrams, new requirements, apologies, or personal opinions like "we think this is a good project."

💡

Start your conclusion with: "This project..." or "In conclusion, [Project Name]..."

Sample Conclusion:

"This project, MeroAttendance, addresses the critical inefficiency of manual student attendance tracking in Nepalese colleges by providing a fast, accurate, and user-friendly QR-code based solution. By eliminating roll call and providing real-time attendance analytics, the system is expected to save over 1 hour of class time per week per teacher while improving attendance data accuracy to above 95%. Future versions will incorporate AI-based attendance pattern analysis and a dedicated mobile application for students, further extending the system's reach and capability."

📚
Section 10
References
List all sources using IEEE citation format. Minimum 5–10 references covering academic papers, books, websites, and similar apps.
IEEE Citation Format Rules

IEEE style uses numbered citations in square brackets — [1], [2], [3] — in the text, and a matching numbered list at the end of the document.

Types of References
📰 Journal / Conference Paper
[1] A. Author, B. Author, "Paper Title," Journal Name, vol. XX, no. X, pp. XXX–XXX, Month Year.
🌐 Website / Online Resource
[2] Organization Name, "Page Title," Year. [Online]. Available: https://www.url.com/page. [Accessed: Month Day, Year].
📱 Application / Software
[3] Company Name, "App Name," Version X.X, Platform, Year. [Online]. Available: https://appstore.com/link.
📘 Book
[4] A. Author, Book Title, Xth ed. City, Country: Publisher Name, Year, pp. XX–XX.
Full Reference List Example
[1] J. Smith and A. Doe, "Design Patterns in Software Engineering," IEEE Trans. Software Eng., vol. 45, no. 3, pp. 120–135, Mar. 2020.

[2] eSewa, "About Us," 2024. [Online]. Available: https://esewa.com.np/about. [Accessed: Apr. 20, 2026].

[3] R. Pressman and B. Maxim, Software Engineering: A Practitioner's Approach, 8th ed. New York, USA: McGraw-Hill, 2015, pp. 45–60.

[4] Google LLC, "Firebase Documentation," 2024. [Online]. Available: https://firebase.google.com/docs. [Accessed: Apr. 18, 2026].

[5] B. Klasnić, "QR Code Attendance Systems in Higher Education," Proc. Int. Conf. on EdTech, pp. 88–94, Jan. 2022.
💡

Where to find references: Google Scholar, IEEE Xplore, ResearchGate, ACM Digital Library, similar apps (eSewa, Hamro Patro), government portals, and official documentation.

⚠️

Never cite Wikipedia as a reference. Use it only to find original sources. Academic proposals require primary or peer-reviewed sources.
